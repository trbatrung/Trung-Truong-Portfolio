# Positioning

The strategic spine of the site. Everything else (design, copy, structure) serves this.
_Revised with Ethan on 2026-10-08. Supersedes the 2026-07-21 blueprint, which is still in git history._

## Who the site is for: both, leadership leads
- **Primary reader:** a hiring manager or founder filling a senior creative leadership role, one
  that owns creative direction across a function. They should leave thinking: *he sets the
  standard other people work to.*
- **Secondary reader:** a client with a production or editing job. The door stays open and easy to
  find. It is no longer the front door.

What changed: the July plan treated editing (then ~90% of revenue) as the front-door hire and
banned any leadership claim. The August brief (`copy-rewrite-brief.md`) moved the target to senior
creative leadership roles. This doc reconciles the two.

## The core position
From *"a producer who sees the picture through both lenses"* to *"someone who owns the pipeline
and sets the standard inside it, including where AI belongs."*

The fusion from July still holds and is still the differentiator. Most creative leads can't show a
documented point of view on tooling. Most AI operators can't run a production. Ethan does both,
and has the systems to show for it.

## The governing rules
1. **Every line says what Ethan decided, not what he did.** Decision verbs (directed, owned, set,
   built, defined) over craft verbs (produced, edited, cut, shot, graded). Test: could the editor
   on that job have written this sentence about themselves? If yes, it's too low.
2. **No claim without proof on the same screen.** Carried over from July. Leadership altitude is
   earned with decisions next to evidence, not with titles.

## Credit rules (never inflate)
- **Confirmed roles (2026-10-08):** all pre-AI work (brand film, client, ads, long-form, motion) was
  edit and planning. The AI systems were full direction, end to end. The site labels every video
  with its role.
- An editing engagement is described by the creative decisions inside the edit. It is never called
  a directed piece.
- Team leadership is past tense (Arrowhead, Mike Dee). Today Ethan is a solo operator at Edge8.
  Never imply a current in-house team.
- No metric or client name that isn't in the verified facts (`profile.md`). Ask rather than fill
  a gap. Failure stories in particular come from Ethan, never from the writer.

## Page structure
**Hero (desktop) → Systems → How I Choose Tools → What I Do + About → Contact.**
- **Hero is the desktop.** One fixed screen (1440x900, 390x950 on phones) scaled to fit, on a
  color or ombre background (no photos). The H1 is read first, the icons second.
- **All work lives on the desktop:** loose files, six folders, and Finder. No video grid on the
  page.
- **Depth lives on the desktop too.** The page carries one line per idea, about 480 words below
  the hero. The full point of view and the full tool tests open as notes.

## The two-layer read (revised)
- **Skim layer (first 5 seconds):** ownership and standard-setting in plain words. No buzzwords.
  A hiring manager sees a lead. A client still sees someone who delivers.
- **Depth layer:** the systems (including what broke), the tool-vetting findings, the POV.

## AI-Augmented Production: systems, not demos
Three systems (code-based animation, avatar video at volume, end-to-end AI lesson). Each one says
what it is, how it runs, and **what broke and what Ethan changed as a result.** The failure line is
the credibility anchor and is not optional.

## Engagement framing ("What I do")
A ladder: **Define** (use case and tooling, including when AI is the wrong call) → **Own** (full
pipeline, script to final master) → **Deliver** (finishing). Editing stays on the ladder as the
finishing layer, and as the open door for clients.

## The POV copy (locked, matches the live site)

> ### Slop or salvation. Both are wrong.
>
> People treat AI video like it's either garbage or magic. Lean too hard either way and you ship
> something bad.
>
> The market doesn't help. Thousands of AI tools, all charging premium fees, each marketed like a
> specialist: Tool A "best for UGC," Tool B "best for long-form." Strip the wrapper and most do the
> same thing. Most of the job is picking the right one for *your* use case, and knowing where it
> breaks **before** you've burned a month and a budget finding out.
>
> So I don't start with a tool. I start with a question: **what is this video actually for?**
> Pushing a product with UGC is a different problem than scaling short-form, and each has a
> different right answer. Give me the use case and I'll test five tools that claim to do the same
> thing and tell you exactly where each one falls apart.
>
> I'll also tell you when *not* to use AI. It's built for scale, not the one hero film that has to
> be perfect. It won't run your whole pipeline, and for most companies it doesn't need to. Knowing
> that line is the value.
>
> AI gets you to 80% fast. Knowing when that 80% is the finish line, instead of chasing a
> perfection that quietly ruins the work, is the difference between shipping and spinning.
>
> The rest is taste. I can usually tell what's wrong with a video in the first few seconds, and I
> know that past a point, fixing it makes it worse.
>
> Most producers see the picture through one lens: AI *or* real production. I see both. That's the
> whole job.
